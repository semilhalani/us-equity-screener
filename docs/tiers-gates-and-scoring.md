# How Tiers, Disqualifier Gates, and Scoring Work Together

This document walks through the three pieces of the pipeline that turn raw fetched data into an actual opinion about a stock: how tickers get assigned a tier, how the five hard pass or fail gates work, and how the scoring engine turns everything into a 0 to 100 score and a verdict.

## Tiers

Every ticker gets assigned to one of four tiers once, during the universe build, from two independent Finviz screener scrapes. The first checks index membership: is this ticker in the S&P 500, the NASDAQ 100, or the Dow Jones 30. The second checks which of Finviz's own six market cap buckets a ticker falls into: Mega, Large, Mid, Small, Micro, or Nano.

Index membership wins first, regardless of market cap. Any ticker in any of those three indexes becomes Tier1_Core, full stop, and its cap bucket never even gets checked. Otherwise, cap bucket decides: Mega, Large, or Mid becomes Tier2_LargeMid, Small becomes Tier3_Small, and Micro or Nano becomes Tier4_Moonshot. If a ticker matched neither an index nor any cap bucket at all, which does happen occasionally when Finviz's screener simply did not return market cap data for it, it defaults to Tier3_Small rather than being silently dropped from the universe.

These are Finviz's own predefined bucket boundaries, not thresholds this system calculates itself. There is a set of dollar figure constants sitting in the configuration file for reference, but they are explicitly not wired into the tiering logic today. Finviz's own bucket labels are what actually drive tiering.

Tiers are a snapshot, not something that updates live. They only get recalculated on a full universe rebuild, so if a company's market cap grows enough to cross into a different bucket, it shows up in its new tier the next time the universe gets rebuilt, not automatically as it happens. The precise dollar market cap figure, fetched separately, does not feed back into tiering at all. Only Finviz's bucket label decides which tier a stock lands in, even though the exact dollar figure is still stored and used elsewhere, like normalizing insider transaction size against market cap.

## Disqualifier gates

Before a stock is ever scored, it has to pass through five checks that ask a fundamentally different kind of question than scoring does. Scoring asks how good a stock is, on a scale. These gates ask whether there is something specifically disqualifying about it, a plain yes or no. A stock can have a genuinely strong looking Fundamentals_Score and still end up disqualified, since a failed gate overrides everything else regardless of how good the rest of the picture looks. QXO is a real example: it scores 65 on Fundamentals_Score, an unremarkable but not bad number, and still ends up marked as disqualified for excessive dilution.

Two real network calls happen per ticker to run these checks: a Finviz page scrape for liquidity, and a Finnhub SEC filings pull for dilution and, for Moonshot tickers, zero revenue and cash runway too. That second call happens at most once per ticker even though up to three different gates need it, cached in memory for the duration of processing that one ticker.

**Liquidity, all tiers.** The question is simple: can this stock actually be bought and sold without a single order moving the price, or getting stuck unable to exit. It is measured as average daily dollar volume, average shares traded times current price. The threshold is one hundred thousand dollars a day for Tier1, Tier2, and Tier3 stocks, but only fifty thousand dollars a day for Moonshot stocks, since moonshot names are expected to be thinner by nature and the bar is deliberately lower rather than disqualifying most of the entire tier on liquidity alone. If Finviz could not be scraped for a ticker, this check is left blank rather than forced to pass or fail.

**Listing age, all tiers.** The question is whether a company has been public long enough to have any real track record. It is measured as days since IPO, computed during the universe build, and fails if that is under one hundred eighty days, roughly six months. Almost everything else in the system depends on trailing history, four quarters of earnings, three year growth rates, none of which exists for a genuine newcomer. One thing worth knowing about how this is coded: if the underlying new listing flag is blank or unknown for any reason, this check defaults to a pass rather than a neutral unknown state, which is a mild inconsistency with how every other gate handles missing data. In practice this rarely matters, since IPO date is reliably fetched during the universe build, but it is worth knowing about rather than assuming it is airtight.

**Dilution, all tiers.** The question is whether a company has been issuing a lot of new shares, shrinking each existing shareholder's slice of future profits. It is measured as the real change in shares outstanding over roughly the last year, pulled directly from two SEC filings at least three hundred thirty days apart, a genuine year over year comparison rather than a few month blip. The threshold fails anything with more than thirty percent growth in shares outstanding. This was checked by hand against real companies before going live. VLO showed a five percent decrease, actually buying back shares, and correctly passed. ANVS showed almost sixty percent growth and was correctly flagged. EVGO showed well over a hundred percent growth and was correctly disqualified. The share count primarily comes from the income statement's weighted average shares line, since every company reports that to compute EPS, falling back to a balance sheet concept for the minority of companies that tag it there instead. If SEC filings do not go back far enough, a newer company, or the concept genuinely cannot be found, this is labeled as insufficient history rather than counted against the ticker.

**Zero revenue quarters, Moonshot tier only.** This counts how many of the last eight reported quarters had essentially no revenue, and it never disqualifies a stock by itself. Being pre revenue is often the entire premise of a legitimate moonshot bet, a biotech still in clinical trials, a company that has not launched its product yet, so penalizing that outright would disqualify the exact kind of company this tier exists to consider. It only contributes to disqualification when paired with a failing cash runway check, since no revenue and running low on cash together signals a real, near term risk of forced, dilutive financing, while either fact alone does not.

**Cash runway, Moonshot tier only.** This is the only gate that touches actual cash, and it disqualifies on its own. The question is how many months until the company runs out of money at its current burn rate. It is measured as cash on the balance sheet divided by a monthly burn rate derived from quarterly operating cash flow, and it fails anything with less than six months of runway. If a company is already cash flow positive, not burning cash at all, it passes automatically with no runway figure reported, since the question does not apply to it. If cash or cash flow figures cannot be found in the filing, this is labeled as insufficient data rather than counted against the ticker.

**Combining the five into one verdict.** A ticker is disqualified if liquidity fails, or listing age fails, or dilution fails, or cash runway fails. Zero revenue never triggers disqualification by itself, only through the cash runway combination described above. The reasons behind a disqualification are listed as short codes, and a real ticker shows two stacked together at once. DFLI failed both dilution and cash runway.

The missing data rule is the same across all five gates, worth stating once instead of five times. If a gate's underlying data could not be found or computed, that gate's result is blank, never forced to a pass or a fail, and it never counts toward disqualification. A ticker cannot be disqualified by a check that was never actually able to run.

## Scoring

Nothing before the scoring step makes a judgment call. Universe, Fundamentals, Earnings, Insiders, and the disqualifier gates are all just fetching and organizing facts. Scoring is the first step that turns those facts into an opinion. It runs once, reads several other sheets, and for every ticker asks two separate questions: is this stock even allowed to be considered, meaning did it pass the five gates, and given everything known, how good is it, and what should happen next.

Scoring never calls an external API. It is pure arithmetic on data that is already sitting in the sheets, which is why it runs in seconds rather than minutes, and why re running it costs nothing against any quota. It also recalculates everything from scratch every time it runs rather than remembering yesterday's score, so the Scores sheet is only ever as current as its four underlying inputs, fundamentals, benchmarks, disqualifier gates, and earnings and insider data. A fresh looking timestamp on a score does not guarantee the underlying facts behind it are fresh too.

Stocks are never compared to the whole market, only to their own tier and industry or sector. A biotech in Tier3_Small gets compared to the median biotech in Tier3_Small, not to a company like Apple. This is why the same PE ratio can score completely differently on two different tickers, since it is judged against realistic peers rather than in absolute terms. Tier1, Tier2, and Tier3 use one formula built from Quality, Value, and Growth. Tier4_Moonshot uses a completely different formula, since asking whether a pre revenue nano cap is a good value stock is close to meaningless, and the real question for that tier is whether there is a legitimate growth story with a real cash cushion behind it, not just a hopeful story. This is also why almost every column is blank for Moonshot tier stocks, and Moonshot_Score is blank for everything else. TURB, a Moonshot tier stock, has blank Quality, Value, Growth, and Fundamentals scores but a real Moonshot_Score of 22.5. SPRY, a Tier3_Small stock, has real Quality, Value, Growth, and Fundamentals scores but a blank Moonshot_Score. Neither is missing data. That is the system working as intended.

**Quality_Score**, computed for Tier1, Tier2, and Tier3 only, asks whether this is a well run, profitable business. It blends how ROE compares to peers at forty five percent weight, how profit margin compares to peers at thirty five percent, and how stable ROE has been over the last three years compared to right now at twenty percent, since wild swings in profitability get penalized even if the current number looks fine. Each underlying comparison scores fifty times the stock's value divided by the peer median, capped between zero and one hundred, so matching the peer median exactly scores fifty and doubling it scores the maximum of one hundred. TPB scores 98.8, dramatically above peer tobacco company medians on both ROE and margin. QXO scores only 20, well below peer industrial distribution medians.

**Value_Score**, also Tier1 through Tier3 only, asks whether a stock is cheap or expensive relative to similar companies, not relative to its own price history, which is a separate signal handled by Entry_Timing on the Buy List instead. It blends PE against the peer median, debt to equity against the peer median, and for Tier1 and Tier2 stocks only, dividend yield, since most smaller companies do not pay one. For PE and debt, lower is better, so the formula flips direction: fifty times two minus the ratio. A PE at exactly the peer median scores fifty, a PE at half the peer median scores seventy five, and a PE at double the peer median scores zero. EVGO scores 81.5 on Value despite being disqualified elsewhere, since it is genuinely cheap by these metrics even though the dilution problem is a separate, harder gate that overrides it.

**Growth_Score**, Tier1 through Tier3 only, asks whether revenue and earnings are actually growing relative to peers, blending three year revenue growth at fifty five percent and three year EPS growth at forty five percent against the peer median. SPRY and ONT both hit the maximum of one hundred, with growth rates far outpacing their peers.

**Fundamentals_Score** is not a fourth independent measurement. It is a weighted average of Quality, Value, and Growth above, with weights that shift by tier. Tier1_Core weights quality highest, since stability matters most for mature companies. Tier2_LargeMid is balanced across all three. Tier3_Small weights growth highest, since smaller companies get bought for growth potential rather than stability. This is the number the verdict thresholds are actually based on.

**Moonshot_Score**, Tier4 only, asks whether there is a real growth story with responsible debt trends behind a speculative micro or nano cap, not just hype. It blends revenue growth against peers at fifty percent, profit margin against peers at thirty percent, and whether debt relative to assets has been improving or worsening over three years at twenty percent, since a company taking on more relative debt every year gets penalized even if today's snapshot looks fine.

**Volatility_Score and Reliability_Score**, computed for every tier, come from the earnings history step rather than from scoring itself. Volatility is a recency weighted average of the absolute size of recent EPS surprises, so a high score just means big swings in either direction. Reliability tracks direction instead, where one hundred means consistent beats and zero means consistent misses. ONT and SABR both hit one hundred on volatility, meaning their earnings reports have historically moved the needle hard.

**Earnings_Archetype_Code** crosses quality with volatility into four buckets, since a volatile stock is not automatically a bad one, it depends on the business underneath it. High quality and low swing is a stable compounder. High quality and high swing is a volatility harvest candidate, a good business with a jumpy stock where overreactions can be entry points. Low quality and high swing is high risk, jumpy and not a great business underneath, the genuinely dangerous combination. Low quality and low swing is low conviction, not exciting in either direction. This is left blank when quality or volatility themselves are blank, since there is nothing to cross if one side does not exist. ONT is a real high risk example, with quality well below the cutoff and volatility at the maximum, a genuinely risky combination correctly flagged.

**Qualified** is the hard gate result carried over from the disqualifier checks. True means a ticker passed all five. False means do not buy it, full stop, regardless of how good the scores above look. RDAG scores 88.8 on Fundamentals_Score but is marked false because of excessive dilution, and a great looking score never overrides a failed gate. Blank means the gates never actually evaluated this ticker, deliberately not defaulted to true, since never checked and checked and passed are genuinely different situations.

**Verdict** is the actual recommendation, and it never overrides Qualified. Disqualification is checked first, before anything else, and a disqualified ticker gets marked accordingly regardless of every other score. For everything else, Moonshot tier stocks get one of three labels off Moonshot_Score, and every other tier gets one of four labels off Fundamentals_Score, from strong candidate down to a weak, avoid label. A ticker gets marked as having insufficient data when the relevant score itself is blank. BIII is a real example, marked as passing the gates but with every score blank, so there is genuinely nothing to base a verdict on.

**Caution_Flags** are caveats layered on top of the verdict, not replacing it. Up to four possible flags can stack: no insider data available at all, heavy insider selling, insider buying support, inconsistent earnings history, which only applies to a strong or watch verdict since a stock already marked weak does not need a separate warning that it is also unreliable, and a volatile and weak fundamentals combination that applies regardless of verdict. ONT stacks two at once, inconsistent earnings and the volatile and weak fundamentals flag.

## Missing data, the full picture

Some fields degrade gracefully. Quality, Value, and Growth each independently skip a missing sub component rather than failing entirely, since the underlying weighted average re normalizes across whatever inputs are actually present. But if a peer benchmark cannot be found at all, that sub component returns a neutral fifty rather than a blank. MAMK is a real example. It has no sector or industry on file, so every benchmark lookup fails, and Quality, Value, and Fundamentals all land at exactly fifty, indistinguishable from genuinely average without checking the underlying data by hand. This is exactly why the Buy List adds an extra check beyond just trusting Fundamentals_Score.

Other fields go genuinely blank rather than faking a number. Moonshot_Score for the wrong tier, Volatility, Reliability, and Archetype when no earnings history exists yet, and Verdict becoming insufficient data when its underlying score is blank. These do not pretend to know something they do not.

A few fields are never blank, by design. Qualified uses an explicit empty value for unknown rather than a fake default, and Disqualify_Reason_Codes, Verdict, and Caution_Flags all carry an explicit code for do not know rather than an empty cell that could mean anything.

The practical takeaway: if a Fundamentals_Score lands somewhere in the forties through sixties, it is worth a quick check of sector and industry before trusting it, since a blank sector or industry is usually the tell that it is a neutral fallback rather than a real assessment.
